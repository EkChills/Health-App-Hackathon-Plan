Health hackathon / Build plan v1 / 2–4 weeks / Web first

BST — the record that reads itself

A photo of a lab report or a prescription goes in. Structured, verified health data comes out — explained in plain language for the patient, and handed to the doctor as a one-page brief.

Architecture thesis — say this line to the judges
The model reads. The rules decide.

AI does extraction and explanation. A deterministic engine makes every judgement that matters — what is out of range, what interacts, what is urgent. That split is the safety story, the credibility, and the thing that separates this from the six other AI health chatbots in the room.

NG

Nigeria first

Everything here assumes a Nigerian patient, a Nigerian lab, and a Nigerian clinic. That is not a limitation — it is the strongest thing about the pitch.

The insight to lead with

A Nigerian patient’s records are scattered across a private lab, a GP, a teaching hospital and a pharmacy that share nothing with each other. So people already carry their own records — a folder, a plastic bag of printouts, photos in a WhatsApp chat. BST is not asking anyone to change their behaviour. It is digitising the folder they already carry. A patient-owned record is not a design compromise here; it is how Nigerian healthcare already works.

Regulation — one slide, and verify it yourself first

  • Nigeria Data Protection Act 2023, regulated by the NDPC. Health data is sensitive personal data requiring explicit consent — exactly what the ShareGrant in §07 provides.
  • NHIA Act 2022 made health insurance mandatory and expanded the HMO market. A policy tailwind worth naming: your future customers are growing because of legislation.
  • Confirm the current wording before you present. A judge who works in the sector will know it better than a summary does.

Who pays here

Out-of-pocket spending dominates Nigerian healthcare, so a paid consumer app is not the business — patients will not pay. Sell B2B2C: private diagnostic lab chains first, then HMOs, then private clinics per seat.

Who you will be compared to

Helium Health owns the hospital EHR layer — sold to facilities, and the institution owns the record. Healthtracka does at-home testing with digital results. Your position: patient-owned and cross-facility. Helium makes one hospital’s records digital; BST makes one person’s records portable across the four places they actually go.

What this changes in the build

  • Android-dominant, and data costs real money. Compress images client-side before upload and tolerate a bad connection. An engineering requirement, not a nicety.
  • Seeded personas get Nigerian names, Nigerian lab formats — PCV, genotype, MP, widal — and Naira wherever money appears.
  • Test extraction on real Nigerian lab printouts, not American samples. The layouts differ, and that difference will break your OCR on stage.
  • Nigerian Pidgin as a plain-language mode. It costs almost nothing — a variation on the explanation prompt you are already writing — and it will land harder with a Nigerian panel than anything else on this list.

Why this wins the room

What they are really askingHow the plan answers it

The climax is the handoff: a crumpled lab photo goes up, ten seconds later there is a clean trend and a plain-language read, the patient taps Share, and the doctor’s screen fills with a clinical brief. Those forty seconds are the entire pitch.

§02

Two users, one shared record

This is not a two-sided marketplace — resist that. It is one record with two views.

Patient — primary

Has documents, cannot read them, forgets what they were told, arrives at appointments unprepared.

“Tell me what this result means and what I should do.”

Clinician — the credibility half

Inherits a patient with no usable history and no time to build one.

“Give me this person’s story in 30 seconds, and don’t make me trust an AI to do it.”

Design the doctor view skeptic-first: every AI-generated sentence is visibly labelled a draft, and every number links back to the document it was read from.

§03

The core loop

01UploadPhoto, PDF, or typed by hand.
02ExtractAI reads the document into structured JSON with a confidence score per field.
03VerifyHuman confirms the fields beside a crop of the source image.
04DecideDeterministic rules engine raises flags.
05ActPatient view, doctor view, share grant.

The verify step is non-negotiable. It costs one screen and it buys the answer to the question every serious judge will ask: what happens when the AI is wrong?

§04

The split

Put this on a pitch slide verbatim. It is the strongest twenty seconds you have.

AI does — assistive only

  • Vision extraction: document image to structured JSON
  • Confidence score per field; low confidence forces human review
  • Plain-language explanation of an already-flagged value
  • Drafts the doctor’s summary from structured fields only

AI never

  • Decides urgency
  • Diagnoses
  • Suggests a dose

If the model hallucinates, a number is wrong and the user sees it next to the source image. The clinical judgement was never the model’s to make.

Rules decide — deterministic, auditable, no model calls

Rule familyWhat it doesSource of truth
Reference rangeFlags high / low / critical, age- and sex-adjustedRange table you seed
TrendRate of change across readings; flags sustained driftYour own math
InteractionDrug–drug interaction checkopenFDA / RxNorm / DDInter
Duplicate therapyTwo drugs, same classDrug class map
Dose sanityAbove max daily doseStatic dose table
Red flagCritical value triggers “seek care now”Hardcoded, human-reviewed
AdherenceDoses logged vs. prescribedArithmetic

Keep the rules engine in its own module with zero model calls inside it, testable with plain fixtures. That is what makes the thesis true rather than a slogan.

Stack: Next.js (App Router, TypeScript), responsive web / PWA so mobile later is mostly free. The skeleton already has one route per screen in §06, and the rules engine lives in lib/rules/.

“But what about a normal doctor consultation, without a report?”

This will come up repeatedly. Split it into three things — two are already done, and the third is a different product.

Already supported

A consult with no documents at all. A patient with zero uploads still has a record: manually entered blood pressure, a symptom note, a medication list typed by hand. The share code works the same, and the doctor brief renders from whatever exists. Manual entry is a must-have precisely so the app is useful before anyone uploads anything. Nothing extra to build.

Cheap to add — async notes

The doctor already writes a note on screen 7. Let the patient see it and reply once. That is a consultation in a thin, defensible sense: advice attached to a record, in writing, with an audit trail. About half a day, since the note already exists as an object. If the team wants “consultation” in the demo, build this version.

Out of scope — live telemedicine

  • WebRTC, scheduling, availability, payments and dispute handling. That is the entire three weeks.
  • It needs doctor identity verification. You cannot connect Nigerian patients to people claiming to be doctors without checking MDCN registration — and a sector judge will ask inside thirty seconds.
  • It does not demo well. Everyone in the room has seen a video call, and bad venue wifi kills it live.
  • Nigeria already has funded telemedicine players. A three-week prototype loses that fight; a three-week prototype that makes records portable is in an open lane.

The resolution: put live consultation on the roadmap slide, not in the build. Your friends get their idea acknowledged, judges see you know where the product goes next, and it costs zero engineering days. “Here’s what we’d build with the sponsorship” is a slide you need anyway.

§07

Consent and the share code

The least obvious piece of the design, and the one doing the most work. Understand it before you build it.

The problem

A patient walks into a clinic. The doctor has never met them and has no account connected to them. How does that doctor see the record for the next twenty minutes, without the patient handing over their password?

Every obvious answer is bad

  • Email a PDF — static, no trends, no flags, and the data now sits in a stranger’s inbox forever with no way to take it back.
  • Give the doctor a login — requires pre-registering every clinic in the country before the app is useful. Dead on arrival.
  • Patient logs in on the doctor’s computer — hands over the whole account, permanently, on someone else’s machine.

The mechanism

Same idea as a shared document link with an expiry date, or a guest wifi password.

01Tap shareFrom the patient’s own record.
02Pick scopeEverything, labs only, or the last six months.
03Set expiryHours, not weeks. 24h is a sensible default.
04CodeK7QM-2PX — read aloud, or shown as a QR.
05Doctor enters itRecord opens. Access dies on schedule.

Behind it is a single database row — a ShareGrant holding the code, the scope, an expiry and a revoked flag. Every time the doctor opens the record you write a line to AuditLog, and the patient later sees “Dr. Adeyemi viewed your labs on 4 Mar at 10:12.”

Why it earns its place

  • The app works on day one. A code works for a walk-in clinic, a referral, a second opinion in another city, an emergency room. No pre-existing relationship, no clinic onboarding, no admin. That is the actual shape of how people see doctors.
  • It is the consent story, made physical. Most teams answer “how do you handle privacy?” with “we encrypt everything.” You get to answer: the patient chooses who sees it, chooses how much, sets an expiry, can revoke it, and sees every access afterward. Demonstrable beats claimed — for about four hours of work.
  • It is the demo transition. You need a believable reason to move from the patient screen to the doctor screen on stage. Without it you are alt-tabbing between two windows and hoping nobody wonders how the doctor got in.
  • The patient stays in control. In a hospital system the institution owns the record. Here the patient owns it and lends it out. That is a real positioning line for the pitch.

Implementation note — say this unprompted to a technical judge

A short code alone is brute-forceable. Three cheap defenses: short expiry measured in hours; rate-limited attempts with a lock after about five failures; and bind on first claim, so once one clinician redeems the code it stops working for everyone else.

Three weeks

Days 1–2
Decide and deploy Lock the wedge. Wireframe seven screens on paper. Write three seeded personas with real clinical arcs. Agree the API contract and stub it so the frontend never waits on the backend. Ship a public URL on day 2 — deployment problems always surface late and always cost more than you budget.
Week 1
The spine Upload → extract → verify → persist. Rules engine v1: reference ranges and red flags. One marker charting end to end. Friday exit criterion: one document goes in and comes out flagged and charted.
Week 2
The handoff Share grant and consent flow. Doctor brief. Interaction and duplicate-therapy checks. Prescription extraction. Audit log.
Week 3
Survive contact Polish empty and error states. Make the seeded numbers real rather than invented — see §13 for how to do that with no clinician on the team. Rehearse the script five times. Record the fallback video. Freeze the code 48 hours before the demo and spend that time only on the pitch.

Four parallel workstreams — put a name on each today

  • A — Extraction pipeline: upload handling, model calls, JSON schema, confidence scoring
  • B — Rules engine and data: range tables, drug data, flag generation, seed personas
  • C — Patient UI: home, upload and verify, timeline, share
  • D — Doctor UI and pitch: brief, notes, deck, script, demo video
§09

Who pays

They are deciding whether to fund you, so this section is not optional garnish.

Out-of-pocket spending dominates Nigerian healthcare, so a paid consumer app is not the business. Patients will not pay — sell to the organisations around them.

  • Private diagnostic lab chains first — Synlab, Clinix, Afriglobal, Union Diagnostics and their peers. They already print reports nobody understands. White-label BST as their results portal: they pay per report to look modern and to kill the “what does this mean?” phone calls.
  • HMOs — Reliance Health, Hygeia, Avon, AXA Mansard. Fewer duplicate tests and better chronic-disease adherence are line items they already track, and the NHIA Act is growing their market for them.
  • Private clinics and hospitals, per seat, justified by minutes saved per consult.

Verify every company named here is still operating and still a fit before you put it on a slide.

Bring one real statistic about your market — a single credible number outperforms a paragraph of adjectives. And get one quote from one real doctor or patient. It is the cheapest credibility in the entire pitch.

§12

Which markers do you support?

A marker is one measurable thing on a report — PCV, fasting blood sugar, genotype. A Nigerian lab printout carries 25 to 40 of them.

Supporting one properly means doing more than displaying the number. Per marker you need every name labs print it under, the units and their conversions, the normal range, where the danger lines sit, plain-language copy for high and for low, and what to advise. That is real work, and it is the part where being wrong can hurt someone. You cannot do forty of these in three weeks.

So support three completely. Every other value gets extracted and displayed faithfully but not interpreted — “Captured from your report. We don’t interpret this one yet.” Honest, deliberate-looking, and read as discipline rather than a gap.

The Nigerian shortcut

Nigerian lab reports print the reference range on the page, right beside the result. So don’t build a range table — extract the range from the document itself and compare the value against the range that lab printed. You never claim clinical authority, you never source ranges you can’t verify, and you get age- and sex-appropriate ranges for free because the lab already applied them.

The three to pick

MarkerWhyInput path
Fasting blood sugarUbiquitous, cheap, constantly ordered; diabetes climbing fastUploaded lab report
PCV / haemoglobinAnaemia is everywhere, and PCV is the term Nigerian labs actually printUploaded lab report
Blood pressureVery high hypertension prevalence — and it is not on a lab reportManual entry

Blood pressure earning its place through manual entry is deliberate: it exercises your second input path live on stage. Malaria and genotype are more culturally recognisable but they are one-off results — nothing to plot on a timeline.

§13

Working without a clinician

There is no doctor on the team. That is a real constraint, not a fatal one — but it has to change how you build, because inventing plausible-looking clinical data is exactly what a judge with a medical background will catch.

  • Take reference ranges from the source document. The big one — it removes your need for clinical authority almost entirely.
  • Build seeded personas from real, redacted reports. Everyone has old test results at home, or family who do. Blank the names, keep the numbers and the layout. You get clinically real values and authentic Nigerian lab formats to test extraction against — which you need anyway.
  • Label the provenance in the UI. “Reference range as printed on your report.” One line turns the weakness into a visible design principle.
  • A pharmacist, nurse or medical student is enough, and far easier to find than a doctor. Thirty minutes over WhatsApp. Keep looking, but don’t block on it.
  • Say it out loud if asked. “We have no clinician, so we designed the system not to need one — we never assert a normal range, we show the one the lab printed.” Far better than a shaky claim of clinical rigour.
